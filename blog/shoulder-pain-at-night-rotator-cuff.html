Why Your Shoulder Hurts More at Night — and What That Usually Means

By the RegenOrtho Palm Beach care team

Most people who call us about a shoulder do not lead with the reaching or the lifting. They lead with sleep. They can get through the day by working around it, and then they lie down and the shoulder starts aching in a way it never does upright.

Night pain is worth paying attention to, because it is one of the few shoulder symptoms that narrows the list. Daytime shoulder pain can come from almost anything. Pain that reliably wakes you up, especially lying on that side, tends to point toward the rotator cuff or toward arthritis in the joint itself, and those two go down different treatment paths.

Why lying down changes things

Standing up, the weight of your arm creates a small amount of space under the bony arch at the top of the shoulder. Lying down, that arrangement changes. The arm drifts, the space narrows, and inflamed tendon tissue that was tolerable all afternoon gets compressed for six straight hours. Add the fact that there is nothing else competing for your attention at 2 a.m. and a shoulder that scored a four all day feels like a seven.

That is why night pain is a signal rather than a mystery. It is telling you something is irritated or degenerating in the tissue, not simply that you overdid it at the gym.

The four things it usually turns out to be

  • Rotator cuff tendinopathy or a partial tear. The most common answer. Reaching overhead or behind your back hurts, and so does lying on it.
  • Shoulder arthritis. Stiffness that is worst first thing in the morning, grinding or catching through the arc of motion, and a comfortable range that keeps shrinking.
  • Instability. Usually after a fall or a dislocation, sometimes years earlier. The shoulder feels loose or apprehensive in certain positions.
  • Overuse from a repetitive sport. Throwing, serving, swimming, paddling. Common in South Florida and often the easiest to reverse if it is caught before the tissue degenerates.

These overlap, and a shoulder can carry two of them at once. That is the entire argument for getting an actual diagnosis rather than starting treatment based on a guess. A cuff problem and an arthritic joint both hurt at night and respond to almost nothing in common.

Getting the diagnosis right first

Our shoulder evaluations combine a focused physical examination with a review of advanced imaging. The exam locates which structure reproduces your pain; the imaging tells us what shape that structure is actually in. Between the two, the answer is usually clear before any treatment is discussed.

Dr. Marc Matarazzo brings more than 23 years of clinical and surgical experience in shoulder reconstruction to that read. He completed his sports medicine and arthroscopy fellowship at Lenox Hill Hospital in New York City, where he served as an assistant team physician to the New York Jets and New York Islanders. In practice, what that experience buys a patient is a straight answer about which category their shoulder falls into.

What can be done short of surgery

A complete rotator cuff tear in an active patient is often a surgical problem, and we will say so plainly when that is the finding. But a great deal of what shows up as night pain is not a complete tear.

Partial tears and tendinopathies frequently improve with guided rehabilitation paired with biologic support. Our regenerative work on shoulders centers on PRP and other orthobiologics delivered under ultrasound guidance, so the material goes into the damaged tissue rather than near it. For chronic tendon problems that have stopped responding to rest, our advanced non-surgical therapies include EPAT shockwave to stimulate blood flow and collagen remodeling, and cold laser to calm the inflammatory side of the picture.

None of these are a substitute for indicated surgical care, and we do not present them that way. They are the middle category between living with it and repairing it, and for a large share of shoulders that middle category is where the answer sits.

When to stop waiting it out

The rough rule: a shoulder that has been waking you up for more than a few weeks, or one where weakness has started showing up alongside the pain, has stopped being a rest-and-see problem. Weakness in particular changes the conversation, because it suggests the tendon is not just irritated but failing mechanically.
