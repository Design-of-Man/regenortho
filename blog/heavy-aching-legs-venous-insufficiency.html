Heavy, Aching Legs by the End of the Day: When It Is Vein Disease and Not Just Age

By the RegenOrtho Palm Beach care team

Almost nobody books a vein appointment because of how their legs feel. They book because of how their legs look. The heaviness, the swelling by five o’clock, the cramps at night — those get filed under getting older, standing too much, the Florida heat, or the weight of the day.

They are usually symptoms. Vein disease is progressive and underdiagnosed, and it produces symptoms well before it produces anything worth looking at in a mirror.

The symptom list nobody connects to veins

  • Legs that feel heavy or achy by the end of the day, better in the morning
  • Swelling around the ankles that comes and goes
  • Night cramps, or restless legs
  • Visible spider or reticular veins, with or without bulging varicose veins
  • Skin changes or a slow-healing spot near the ankle

The timing is the tell. Venous symptoms are gravity-dependent, so they build through the day and ease overnight. Arterial and musculoskeletal problems do not usually follow that pattern. If your legs are consistently worse at 6 p.m. than at 8 a.m., that is a circulation-return question.

The last item on that list is the one to move on quickly. Skin changes near the ankle indicate the venous problem has been going on long enough to affect the tissue, and that stage is meaningfully harder to reverse than the one before it.

What is actually going wrong

The veins in your legs move blood upward against gravity, and they rely on one-way valves to keep it from falling back down between steps. When those valves stop closing properly, blood pools. Pressure builds in the vein wall. The vein stretches, which pulls its own valves further apart, which lets more blood back down.

That loop is called reflux, and it is the underlying disease. Bulging varicose veins are what the loop looks like from outside after it has been running for years. Which leads to the single most useful thing to understand about vein treatment.

Why some vein treatments do not last

Treating the visible veins without finding the underlying reflux is the reason so many treatments done elsewhere come back. You can close a surface vein and get a good cosmetic result, and if the failing valve upstream is still failing, the pressure simply finds another path. The patient concludes vein treatment does not work. What actually happened is that the branch was treated and the source was not.

So every vein plan at our clinic starts with duplex ultrasound mapping to locate the failing valves before anything is treated. It is not a formality and it is not upselling. It is the step that decides whether the result holds.

What treatment looks like

All of it is minimally invasive and office-based:

  • Endovenous laser or radiofrequency ablation for diseased saphenous veins — closing the source of the reflux from inside.
  • Sclerotherapy for spider and reticular veins.
  • Micro-incision phlebectomy for surface varicosities.
  • Specialized wound care when venous insufficiency has already affected the skin.

Treated veins are closed permanently. New veins can still develop over time, which is what structured follow-up and prevention strategies are for — that is a different thing from a treatment failing.

The insurance question

Worth knowing before you decide this can wait: medical vein care, meaning treatment of symptoms and circulation problems, is often covered. Purely cosmetic treatment is usually elective. We verify your benefits before treatment rather than after, so the financial picture is settled up front.

Where to start

If your legs are heavy by dinnertime, or you have been managing ankle swelling by elevating and hoping, the useful first step is a map rather than an opinion.
